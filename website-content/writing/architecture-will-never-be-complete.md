# Architecture Will Never Be Complete
date: 2026-09-29

*Why timelessness lives not in buildings, but in the endless correction of their errors*

We usually call a building timeless because it seems not to change: the same stones, the same beauty, the same meaning across centuries. I think this gets timelessness exactly backwards. To see why, we first need to ask how a space comes to mean anything at all.

How does a space come to mean anything? A wall, a window or a colonnade does not carry its meaning around inside it. Meaning arises when a mind notices a regularity in space and conjectures a theory that explains it. David Deutsch makes a similar point about Roman numerals in *The Beginning of Infinity*: much of the knowledge needed to calculate with them lived not in the numerals but in the people using them. Architecture works the same way. The building consists of the regularities; we supply the theories, and those theories are themselves conjectures, open to criticism and replacement. This noticed pattern in space is filtered through an explanation that exists in our minds that helps us understand its meaning. This correspondence between a pattern in space and a theory that explains it is what I will call an isomorphism. And because our capacity to conjecture new theories has no fixed limit (our explanatory reach is infinite; read *the farthest reaches* for more on this), the meanings a given space can take in our minds have no fixed limit either. In layman’s terms, we can imagine anything, and because we can imagine it, we can understand it and, if not bounded by the laws of physics, create it.

How do these meanings that correspond to what there is arise? Walls, ceilings, windows and pillars – these are synonymous with the numbers 0 to 9. The base of our formal system. Patterns or arrangements of these elements can constitute several different meanings according to our best explanation of *reality*. Here is the first link between architecture and the philosophy of science. Namely, that this meaning is a physical process of the replication of knowledge from the physical substrate, say a wall, to another physical substrate (the human brain), and the subsequent computation of that knowledge which is filtered through our best explanations of the world, both being selected for against tests and criticism, closely resembling the process of *Darwinian natural selection*, hence introducing our second link to the fundamentals *of reality*.

This intense analogy of the selection of our best explanatory theories of reality and the biological process of life, as *David Duestch* says, is no coincidence, as both these processes share a common principle of life, namely knowledge conservation. In the case of natural selection, it is genetic knowledge encoded in DNA, and in the case of theory selection, it is explanatory knowledge, a process unique to humans (specifically as described by *Karl Popper*).

This fact can help us draw the conclusion that giving a particular space a particular meaning is a matter of computation, and it follows that this computation will change as our theories of life (the software that the computation is filtered through, namely our brain) evolve. This is where, in my view, “timeless architecture” is instantiated. In other words, a particular physical space that does not physically change but is perceived differently over time, adjusting to a new function and providing a new atmosphere, almost perfectly fitting the new epoch’s needs. This is impossible, as at the initial instant, we had a different explanatory theory of what that space means and, hence, how it makes us feel. As time went on, as we conjectured and criticised our way through life and refuted that first explanatory theory, we now came to a new explanatory theory of the same regularities (the same space) which alters the software (the human mind), resulting in a perception of space, therefore giving a different meaning of that new space. It is my belief that if a space is designed with the criterion of being a hard-to-vary explanation (filter of computation) for each component of its design, timeless function, timeless beauty and timeless “goodness” emerge from within. There will, however, come a time where our explanations have evolved to a degree where the current regularity of the designed space is no longer a hard-to-vary explanation; that is to say, we will be able to imagine other configurations of space that explain its function with ever more elegance and nuance.

This essay contradicts the typical meaning of the phrase “timeless architecture”, which gives false emphasis to the unchanging nature of a timeless piece of art; on the contrary, a timeless piece of art is an extremely adaptive, ever-changing but always perfectly fitting method of knowledge conservation.

When given the room to be adaptable to fit just perfectly into our best explanations of the world, there is no limit to what these regularities or patterns of space can correspond to. Due to its universality, coupled with the explanatory universality of the human brain, there is fundamentally no limit to the number of adaptations or types of adaptations that architecture can embody. Ever-changing, ever-correcting adaptation being the focal point of timeless architecture, it follows that it is impossible for the same building and therefore the same regularities to be timelessly relevant forever; there will come a time when they don’t fit into our explanations, and thus we can draw the conclusion that setting a METHOD OF CONSTANT CHANGE BY WAY OF NATURAL SELECTION, that is, selection of the spaces that have not been refuted by the current best software (the best theories of the world available in our minds, which in turn should ALSO BE SUBJECT TO CONSTANT CHANGE BY METHOD OF CONJECTURING EXPLAINATIONS AND CRITICISING THEM). Furthermore, it follows that TIMELESS ARCHITECTURE is not an idea of the subject matter, namely the physical space itself, but rather the process or method of change that the space is subject to. It is this method that architects concerned with architectural progress should pursue, and it is this method that is TIMELESS. We can always get closer to the method but never quite grasp it in its entirety. For if we did, there would be no DOING architecture anymore, only MAKING architecture.

Nonetheless, my framing above may make it seem as though beauty is subjective, as Margaret Hungerford said, “Beauty is in the eye of the beholder,” but that is not the case. Beauty is OBJECTIVE and compatible with an ever-changing view, as posed in this essay. I will be writing more on the objective reality of beauty, but for the purposes of this essay, it is enough to say that there is an objective beauty that an ever-changing METHOD of design AND DESIGN ITSELF needs to be adaptable enough to strive towards; however, it would never reach, for the same reason we can never quite grasp the ENTIRE METHOD. In a sense there is a gap in EVERYTHING that is objective, being at the edge of infinity. This gap is itself discoverable through the evolution of knowledge-bearing ideas, which will in turn reveal new gaps on our path to infinity. Architecture will never be complete, but the physical process of doing architecture should be one of continuous errors, for if there aren’t errors, they cannot be corrected and progress cannot be made.
